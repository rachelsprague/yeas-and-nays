About

About & methodology

Yeas & Nays publishes every recorded vote in the U.S. House and Senate, taken from the official record and shown without commentary. It does not endorse candidates, parties or positions.

Data sources

Every vote and member record comes from a public, official or widely used source. Each vote page links to its original record.

House roll call votes
Office of the Clerk, U.S. House of Representatives, clerk.house.gov
Senate roll call votes
United States Senate, senate.gov
Members: names, parties, states, districts and terms
congress-legislators, an open dataset maintained by the @unitedstates project, github.com/unitedstates/congress-legislators
Members: official links
Each current member's official website, contact form, Washington office and phone, and official social media accounts, from the same congress-legislators dataset. Profiles show the X, Facebook, Instagram, YouTube, Mastodon and Bluesky accounts it lists; it also lists accounts on other services, such as Threads, which profiles do not show.
Bills: sponsors, summaries and text
Each bill's sponsor (the member who introduced it), introduced date, number of cosponsors and latest action, and the lists of bills each member introduced and cosponsored, come from congress.gov. Summaries are written by the Congressional Research Service, Library of Congress, and shown exactly as published, labeled with the version of the bill they describe. Not every measure has one. Each vote page links to the measure's full text on Congress.gov. A measure's path, from introduction to law, comes from its actions on Congress.gov, which the Library of Congress records days after they happen, and from the recorded votes on this site. The lists of bills each member introduced and cosponsored are kept for each Congress on this site: a profile shows the member's latest Congress first, with each earlier one collapsed below it. Cosponsored lists are fetched over several hourly updates, so a member's profile shows its Bills cosponsored section once that member's list is first fetched. Likewise, a profile shows its Bills introduced section once that member's list of bills introduced has been fetched.
Floor schedule ("Coming up")
House: the Majority Leader's weekly floor schedule, docs.house.gov, which lists measures that may be considered that week. Senate: cloture motions filed and not yet voted on, from the Senate's daily floor activity, and the time the Senate next convenes, from senate.gov. Neither is a promise: items are often moved or dropped. See the floor schedule.
Floor band (home page)
While a chamber is in session, the home page links its official live video and floor log. When the House convened, and when each chamber next convenes, come from the House Clerk's floor proceedings and the Senate's session days on senate.gov. The site is updated hourly, so the band can show up to an hour after a chamber adjourns. The Senate's line shows from the time it is scheduled to convene until senate.gov records its adjournment. Either chamber's line also drops 14 hours after the time it shows, in case the data stops updating. A line reads "In session" once the data shows the chamber convened, and "Scheduled session" when it shows only the scheduled time, as the Senate's line does, since senate.gov records a convening only after the Senate adjourns. The Senate's pro forma sessions are left out.
Members: biographies
The "Before Congress" section of a member's profile comes from the newest edition of the Congressional Directory, published by the Joint Committee on Printing on govinfo.gov. Each biography is shown as printed; the directory notes that biographies are furnished or authorized by each member's office. The directory comes out a few times each Congress, so members who took office after the newest edition have no biography until the next one.
Portraits
Official congressional portraits collected by the @unitedstates project, github.com/unitedstates/images. Members without a portrait show their initials.
Updates
The site checks for new votes every hour.

Definitions

Recorded vote
A vote in which each member's position is recorded by name. Voice votes and unanimous consent are not included, because no individual positions are recorded. The ways each chamber votes are described in How the House and Senate vote.
Yea, Nay, Not Voting
Positions as recorded. The House records some votes as Aye and No; they appear here as recorded and count the same as Yea and Nay. Present is shown as Present and counts toward neither side.
Party majority
The position, Yea or Nay, taken by more than half of a party's members who voted Yea or Nay on that roll call. If a party splits evenly, it has no majority on that vote.
Voted against party majority
A member who voted Yea or Nay opposite their party's majority. Not Voting and Present never count as voting against.
Independents
Independents are grouped with the party they caucus with when party majorities are calculated. They are still labeled I everywhere on the site.
Missed votes
Roll calls marked Not Voting, as a share of all roll calls held in the member's chamber while they were in office. Each Congress is counted on its own: a profile shows the member's latest Congress, with earlier ones in a table below, and never combines figures across Congresses. The rules on voting are described in How missed votes work.
Voted with party majority
The share of a member's Yea and Nay votes that matched their party's majority. Votes where the party had no majority are left out.
Terms
A profile counts a member's terms in their chamber across every Congress, from congress-legislators, including the current one. A partial term, after a special election or an appointment, counts as one term. "First term began" gives the year the earliest of them began.
Chamber median
The middle value among all members of the House, or of the Senate, who had at least a tenth as many roll calls in the current Congress as the member of that chamber with the most: half had a higher rate and half a lower one. The minimum leaves out members who were in office for only a small share of the chamber's roll calls. It appears under a member's missed votes and party majority figures for comparison.
Policy area
The single subject the Congressional Research Service assigns to each bill, such as Health or Taxation. A member's profile counts the bills they introduced and cosponsored in their latest Congress by policy area. New bills may not have one yet.

How votes work

Many recorded votes are not on whether to pass a measure. They decide how the chamber handles it: whether to take it up, end debate, change it, or send it to a committee. Each vote page states the official question and, under the result, what that kind of vote decides. The result always refers to that question. For example, "Motion to Refer: Passed" means the motion to send the measure to a committee was agreed to, not that the measure itself passed.

Some motions, such as motions to refer or table, are offered while a measure is pending on the floor. If one is agreed to, the measure is set aside or sent to committee without a vote on the measure itself.

How parties and votes are shown

  • Parties are written as text (D, I, R) and always listed alphabetically.
  • Every party gets the same style and emphasis. The site uses no party colors.
  • Positions are always written out. In charts, Yea is solid, Nay is striped and Not Voting is open, so they read the same in black and white.
  • The floor activity charts color kinds of vote (passage, amendments, nominations, procedural and other), never parties, and list every count in a table.
