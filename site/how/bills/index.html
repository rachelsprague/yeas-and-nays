How things work

How a bill becomes law

Congress votes on four kinds of measures. Only two of them, bills and joint resolutions, can become law. To do so, a bill must pass the House and the Senate in identical form and then go to the President.

Kinds of measures

"Measure" is the general word for a bill or resolution. Each has a prefix that shows its kind and the chamber where it was introduced, followed by a number.

Bill (H.R., S.)
The form used for most legislation. A bill becomes law after both chambers pass it in identical form and it goes to the President, as described below.
Joint resolution (H.J.Res., S.J.Res.)
There is little practical difference between a bill and a joint resolution, and joint resolutions become law the same way bills do. One exception: a joint resolution proposing an amendment to the Constitution needs two-thirds of each chamber, is not presented to the President, and is sent to the states. It becomes part of the Constitution when three-fourths of the states ratify it.
Concurrent resolution (H.Con.Res., S.Con.Res.)
Used for matters affecting the operations of both chambers. Both the House and the Senate must agree to it. It is not presented to the President and does not become law.
Simple resolution (H.Res., S.Res.)
Used for a matter concerning one chamber alone. Only that chamber votes on it, and it is not presented to the President. In the House, a "rule" setting the terms of debate for a bill is a simple resolution.

The path of a bill

A bill can start in either chamber, except that the Constitution requires bills for raising revenue to start in the House (see How the House and Senate differ). Joint resolutions follow the same steps.

  1. Introduced. A member introduces the bill, and it is given its number. It is referred to the committee with jurisdiction over its subject.
  2. Committee. The committee may hold hearings, where members hear from witnesses, and a "markup" session, where they vote on amendments to the bill. The committee can then report the bill to the full chamber, with or without amendments, or take no further action on it. Under certain circumstances a bill can be brought to the floor without a committee report.
  3. Passes the first chamber. The full chamber debates the bill, votes on amendments and other motions, and then votes on passage.
  4. Passes the second chamber. The other chamber may refer the bill to one of its own committees, then debates and votes on it in the same way.
  5. Differences resolved. A bill must pass both chambers in the same form. If the second chamber changes the text, the bill goes back to the first, which can accept the changes or propose others. Both chambers can instead appoint members to a conference committee, which settles the differences and reports one version back to both chambers for a final vote.
  6. To the President. Once both chambers have passed the bill in identical form, it is printed in final form ("enrolled") and presented to the President, who can:
    • sign it, and it becomes law;
    • veto it, returning it with objections to the chamber where it started;
    • not return it within ten days (Sundays excepted), and it becomes law without a signature; or
    • not return it when Congress's adjournment prevents its return, and it does not become law. This is called a pocket veto.
  7. Veto override. A vetoed bill becomes law if two-thirds of each chamber vote to pass it again, starting with the chamber where it began. The Constitution requires these votes to be recorded by yeas and nays.

Resolutions take shorter paths. A simple resolution is introduced, may go to committee, and is decided by a vote of its one chamber. A concurrent resolution needs both chambers but ends there, without going to the President.

Where the votes on this site fit

This site covers recorded (roll call) votes taken on the House and Senate floor. Committee votes and voice votes are not included (see How the House and Senate vote). A measure can get many recorded votes along its path: on a rule, on amendments, on motions to send it back to committee, on passage in each chamber, on resolving differences, and on overriding a veto. Each vote page states the official question, and the result applies to that question. See How votes work.
