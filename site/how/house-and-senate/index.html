How things work

How the House and Senate differ

A bill must pass both chambers to become law, but the Constitution gives each chamber its own membership and some powers of its own. It also says "Each House may determine the Rules of its Proceedings," so the two chambers debate, amend and vote under different rules.

Members and terms

House of Representatives
The number of voting representatives is fixed by law at no more than 435, divided among the states by population. Representatives are elected every two years, so every seat is up for election at the same time. A representative must be at least 25 years old and a U.S. citizen for at least seven years. A vacant seat is filled by election.
Senate
Each state has two senators, whatever its population, for 100 in all. Senators are elected for six years, and about one-third of seats are up for election every two years. A senator must be at least 30 years old and a U.S. citizen for at least nine years. A vacant seat is filled by election, and a state's legislature may let its governor appoint a senator until then.

A member of either chamber must live in the state they represent when elected.

The House also has five delegates, from the District of Columbia and four territories, and a resident commissioner from Puerto Rico. They are not counted among the voting representatives. House rules let them vote in committees and in the Committee of the Whole, where the House considers many bills and amendments (Rule III, clause 3). If their votes decide a recorded vote in the Committee of the Whole, the question is put again to the House (Rule XVIII, clause 6(h)).

Powers of each chamber

These differences come from the Constitution:

Revenue bills
"All Bills for raising Revenue shall originate in the House of Representatives; but the Senate may propose or concur with Amendments as on other Bills" (Article I, Section 7).
Nominations
The President appoints ambassadors, Supreme Court justices and other officers with the "Advice and Consent of the Senate" (Article II, Section 2). The Senate votes on these nominations; the House does not.
Treaties
The President makes treaties with the advice and consent of the Senate, "provided two thirds of the Senators present concur" (Article II, Section 2).
Impeachment
The House has "the sole Power of Impeachment," which is the power to bring charges. The Senate has "the sole Power to try all Impeachments," and conviction needs two-thirds of the senators present (Article I, Sections 2 and 3).

Who presides

The House elects its Speaker, who presides over it (see Election of the Speaker). In the Senate, the Vice President is President of the Senate and may vote only to break a tie. The Senate also elects a President pro tempore, who serves in the Vice President's absence and may name another senator to preside (Senate Rule I).

Rules on the floor

Debate

A House member may speak for no more than one hour on a question, with exceptions in the rules (House Rule XVII, clause 2), and the House can end debate by ordering the previous question (House Rule XIX, clause 1). Senate rules set no general limit on how long a senator may speak, though a senator may not speak more than twice on a question on the same legislative day without the Senate's leave (Senate Rule XIX). To limit debate, the Senate can agree by unanimous consent or invoke cloture (Senate Rule XXII). See also Filibuster.

Amendments

In the House, an amendment must be on the subject of the measure it would change:

"No motion or proposition on a subject different from that under consideration shall be admitted under color of amendment." (House Rule XVI, clause 7)

An amendment that meets this test is called germane. The House can waive the requirement, for example by adopting a rule. The Senate generally has no such requirement. Its rules require germane amendments in certain cases, including after cloture is invoked (Senate Rule XXII) and on general appropriation bills (Senate Rule XVI).

Bringing a measure to a vote

The House often sets the terms for considering a bill by adopting a rule, a resolution from its Committee on Rules. It can also pass a measure under suspension of the rules, with 40 minutes of debate and a two-thirds vote (House Rule XV, clause 1). The Senate often sets terms by unanimous consent, or votes on a motion to proceed to a measure.

Recorded votes

House members generally vote by electronic device. In the Senate, a clerk calls the names of senators. See How the House and Senate vote.

What this means on this site

Some kinds of votes occur in only one chamber. For example, only the House votes on rules from its Committee on Rules, and only the Senate votes on cloture and nominations. Each vote page states the official question and what that kind of vote decides. See How votes work.
