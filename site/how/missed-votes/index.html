How things work

How missed votes work

The rules of both chambers expect members to vote on each question. A member who does not answer a roll call is recorded as Not Voting, and the record does not say why.

{% include share.html %}

The rules

Rule III, clause 1 of the Rules of the House of Representatives reads:

"Every Member shall be present within the Hall of the House during its sittings, unless excused or necessarily prevented, and shall vote on each question put, unless having a direct personal or pecuniary interest in the event of such question."

Clause 2 of the same rule says a member may not authorize any other person to cast their vote or record their presence.

Rule VI, paragraph 2 of the Standing Rules of the Senate reads:

"No Senator shall absent himself from the service of the Senate without leave."

Under Senate Rule XII, each senator answers the roll call "unless excused by the Senate". A senator who declines to vote gives their reasons, and the Senate decides without debate whether to excuse them. A senator may also decline to vote on a matter when they believe voting would be a conflict of interest.

What the record shows

The roll call record lists each member's position. A member who does not answer is listed as Not Voting. The record does not give a reason, and it does not distinguish an excused absence from any other.

Not Voting is different from Present. A member who votes Present was there and answered the roll call, but took neither side. Neither position counts toward the Yeas or the Nays.

In the Senate, no senator may vote after the presiding officer announces the result (Rule XII).

How this site counts

A member's missed votes are the roll calls where they are recorded as Not Voting, as a share of all roll calls held in their chamber while they were in office. Each Congress is counted on its own. See Missed votes under Definitions.
