How things work

Opening prayers and the chaplains

The rules of both the House and the Senate provide for a prayer by the chaplain at the start of the day's proceedings. Each chamber elects its own chaplain, and guest chaplains give the prayer on some days.

What the rules say

House of Representatives
"The Chaplain shall offer a prayer at the commencement of each day's sitting of the House" (Rule II, clause 5). The prayer comes first in the House's daily order of business, before the Journal and the Pledge of Allegiance (Rule XIV).
Senate
The day's session opens with "the prayer by the Chaplain," followed by the Pledge of Allegiance and then the Journal (Rule IV). When the Senate is in continuous session, the presiding officer suspends business at noon each day for the prayer.

The prayer itself is not put to a vote.

How the chaplains are chosen

The chaplain is an officer of each chamber. Both chambers elect chaplains "as individuals and not as representatives of any religious body or denominational entity," in the words of the Congressional Research Service (CRS).

House of Representatives
The House elects its chaplain at the beginning of each Congress, for a two-year term, usually in the resolution that elects its other officers. If the office becomes vacant, the Speaker may make a temporary appointment, and the House may elect a replacement at any time.
Senate
The Senate chaplain does not have to be reelected when a new Congress begins. When there is a vacancy, the Senate chooses a new chaplain by adopting a resolution.

Besides giving the prayer, the chaplains counsel members, their families and staff, coordinate religious studies and prayer meetings, and schedule guest chaplains. Their offices are funded in the annual legislative branch appropriations acts.

Guest chaplains

Members of the House and Senate invite guest chaplains to give an opening prayer. The House chaplain's office lists the House's guest chaplains since 2001, with the member who sponsored each one where there was a sponsor.

Guest chaplains have come from many faiths. For example, the Congressional Record shows an imam giving the Senate's opening prayer in 2001 and a Hindu guest chaplain giving it on July 12, 2007.

History

The Continental Congress opened its sessions with a prayer and elected a chaplain, Jacob Duché, who served from 1774 to 1776. The Senate elected its first chaplain, Samuel Provoost, on April 25, 1789, and the House elected its first, William Linn, on May 1, 1789.

In the 1850s, petitions asked Congress to abolish the offices. For a short period late in that decade, neither chamber elected a chaplain, and local clergy were invited to serve without pay. Both chambers then returned to electing chaplains.

What courts have ruled

The First Amendment says "Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion, or prohibiting the free exercise thereof." Its first part is called the Establishment Clause. According to CRS, the Supreme Court has not ruled specifically on whether the House and Senate chaplains are constitutional. These decisions concern prayer at the opening of legislative meetings:

Marsh v. Chambers (1983)
The Supreme Court held that the Nebraska Legislature's practice of opening sessions with a prayer by a chaplain paid by the state did not violate the Establishment Clause. The opinion discussed the history of prayer in Congress since 1789.
Town of Greece v. Galloway (2014)
The Supreme Court held that a New York town's practice of opening its board meetings with a prayer did not violate the Establishment Clause. It asked whether the practice "fits within the tradition long followed in Congress and the state legislatures."
Barker v. Conroy (2019)
A member of the House asked its chaplain to invite an atheist to give a secular invocation, and the chaplain declined. The U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit affirmed the dismissal of the resulting lawsuit. It accepted the House's interpretation of its own rules as requiring a religious prayer, and held that "the House does not violate the Establishment Clause by limiting its opening prayer to religious prayer." It did not decide whether excluding a person for being an atheist would differ, under the Constitution, from excluding a nonreligious prayer.
