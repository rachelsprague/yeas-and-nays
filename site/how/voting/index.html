How things work

How the House and Senate vote

Each chamber can decide a question in several ways. Only a recorded vote, also called a roll call vote, lists how each member voted. This site covers recorded votes only.

Voice vote

The presiding officer can put a question to a voice vote, with members answering together. Rule I, clause 6 of the Rules of the House of Representatives gives the form:

"Those in favor (of the question), say 'Aye.'"; and after the affirmative voice is expressed, "Those opposed, say 'No.'"

In the Senate, the presiding officer asks those in favor to say "yea" and those against to say "nay." In both chambers the presiding officer announces which side prevailed. The Senate describes it this way: "In a voice vote, the names of the senators and the tally of votes are not recorded."

Many questions are also settled without any vote, by unanimous consent, when no member objects to a request.

Division vote

If the outcome of a voice vote is in doubt, a division (or standing) vote can be taken. Under House Rule XX, clause 1(a), the House divides if the Speaker is in doubt or a division is demanded: "Those in favor of the question shall first rise or otherwise indicate from their seats and be counted, and then those opposed." In the Senate, a senator in doubt about the outcome of a voice vote may request a division, and the presiding officer counts the senators on each side. A division gives a count for each side; it does not record how each member voted.

Recorded vote

The Constitution lets a minority of members require a recorded vote. Article I, Section 5 reads:

"the Yeas and Nays of the Members of either House on any question shall, at the Desire of one fifth of those Present, be entered on the Journal."

In the House

A member may request a recorded vote, and the vote is taken if the request is supported by at least one-fifth of a quorum (Rule XX, clause 1(b)). In the Committee of the Whole House on the state of the Union, where the House considers many bills and amendments, the request needs the support of at least 25 members (Rule XVIII, clause 6(e)).

Members vote by electronic device unless the Speaker directs another method. The Clerk publishes the names of the members voting on each side and those answering Present. The minimum time for a vote is generally 15 minutes. The Speaker may reduce it to two minutes for a vote that follows another electronic vote or a report from the Committee of the Whole, and the rules allow other exceptions (Rule XX, clauses 2 and 9). On a tie, the question is lost (Rule XX, clause 1(c)).

In the Senate

The Senate takes a roll call vote if one-fifth of a quorum of senators request it. Under Senate Rule XII, the names of senators are called alphabetically, and each senator declares "his assent or dissent to the question." The clerk calls the names and records the votes. No senator may vote after the presiding officer announces the decision, but a senator may change or withdraw a vote, for sufficient reasons, with unanimous consent.

Article I, Section 3 of the Constitution makes the Vice President the President of the Senate, who "shall have no Vote, unless they be equally divided." The Vice President can vote only to break a tie.

When a recorded vote is required

Some questions always get a recorded vote, without a request:

  • Veto overrides. Under Article I, Section 7 of the Constitution, "the Votes of both Houses shall be determined by yeas and Nays, and the Names of the Persons voting for and against the Bill shall be entered on the Journal of each House respectively."
  • Certain House passage votes. Under House Rule XX, clause 10, the yeas and nays are considered ordered on passage of a bill or joint resolution, or adoption of a conference report, making general appropriations or increasing federal income tax rates, and on final adoption of a budget resolution or its conference report.
  • A House vote without a quorum. Under House Rule XX, clause 6, when a quorum fails to vote on a question and a member objects for that reason, the yeas and nays on that question are considered ordered.

What this site covers

This site covers every recorded vote taken on the House and Senate floor, from the House Clerk's and the Senate's roll call records. Voice votes, division votes and questions settled by unanimous consent are not included, because no individual positions are recorded. A measure that passed by voice vote or unanimous consent, with no recorded vote, has no vote page here. When a measure has a recorded vote at one step and not at another, its vote page's bill path, from Congress.gov's record of actions, still shows both steps. See Definitions.
